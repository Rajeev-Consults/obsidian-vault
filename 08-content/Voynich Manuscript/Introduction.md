# 1. What exactly is the Voynich Manuscript?

Its formal Yale designation is **Beinecke MS 408**. It is a richly illustrated **vellum codex**, now held by Yale University's Beinecke Rare Book and Manuscript Library.

It acquired its popular name from **Wilfrid Michael Voynich**, the Polish-American antiquarian bookseller who brought it to public attention in 1912.

Yale describes it as having been produced in **Central Europe around the end of the 15th or during the 16th century**, while radiocarbon analysis of the vellum gives a much tighter date for the parchment itself: approximately **1404–1438 at 95% probability**. [Yale Library](https://beinecke.library.yale.edu/beinecke/collections/beinecke-cipher-voynich-manuscript?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That latter point is crucial: **radiocarbon dates the animal skin, not the moment the ink was put on it.** It strongly establishes an early-15th-century physical substrate, but technically does not prove the exact year of writing. [Voynich Manuscript](https://voynich.nu/extra/carbon.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### What about Roger Bacon?

This is one of the manuscript's great historical myths.

Voynich himself promoted the idea that the manuscript might have been written by **Roger Bacon**, the 13th-century English philosopher and scientist.

That is now essentially untenable. Bacon died in 1292, while the parchment is from the 15th century.

So the romantic Bacon theory has effectively collapsed.

---

# 2. Who wrote it?

**We don't know.**

And this is not simply because nobody has deciphered the text.

We don't even know:

- the author's name
- the language
- the cultural/linguistic community
- the precise geographical origin
- whether there was one author
- whether the text was copied from an earlier source
- whether one person wrote all of it
- whether the illustrator and scribe were the same person

There is evidence for **different scribal hands/textual varieties**. Prescott Currier's work in the 1970s famously identified two statistically and graphically distinct varieties, now called **Currier A and Currier B**. [Voynich Manuscript](https://www.voynich.nu/extra/curr_main.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That does **not** necessarily mean two languages or two authors. It may represent scribes, dialects, textual genres, stages of composition, or something else.

So I'd currently describe the authorship as:

> **Unknown author(s), probably working in a late-medieval European intellectual/medical context, but with the precise cultural setting unresolved.**

---

# 3. How big is it?

This is where the Voynich becomes physically interesting.

The Yale catalogue gives approximately:

**102 vellum folios + 1 paper leaf**

with a physical size of roughly:

**225 × 160 mm**

— about **9 × 6¼ inches**. [Yale Library Search](https://search.library.yale.edu/archives/11167038?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

But if you hear people say **“about 240 pages,”** that isn't necessarily contradictory.

A folio is a physical leaf with two sides, so:

> 102 folios ≈ 204 sides/pages

and some of the manuscript's elaborate folded structures make page-counting more complicated.

There are also **missing leaves**.

The manuscript originally appears to have been considerably larger. Some estimates put the surviving manuscript at roughly **240 surviving pages/images**, depending on how the foldouts and missing material are counted.

It was constructed in **20 quires**, using a binding structure characteristic of medieval European manuscripts. Some quires contain unusually elaborate foldouts. [Yale University Press](https://yalebooks.yale.edu/2016/11/21/designing-the-indecipherable-the-voynich-manuscript/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That physical structure itself is evidence that this wasn't simply someone scribbling nonsense into a notebook.

---

# 4. What are the “chapters”?

This is an important qualification.

The manuscript **doesn't have chapter headings saying “Chapter 1,” “Chapter 2,” etc.**

Scholars conventionally divide it into **six major sections according to the illustrations**.

### The six conventional sections

|Section|What we see|
|---|---|
|**1. Herbal**|Plants, roots, leaves, flowers|
|**2. Astronomical / Astrological**|Stars, zodiac signs, celestial diagrams|
|**3. Balneological**|Human figures, pools, baths, tubes and channels|
|**4. Cosmological**|Large circular diagrams and elaborate foldouts|
|**5. Pharmaceutical**|Plants, roots, containers/jars|
|**6. Recipes / text**|Dense paragraphs, frequently marked by star-like symbols|

Yale itself describes essentially these six groupings. [Yale Library](https://beinecke.library.yale.edu/beinecke/collections/beinecke-cipher-voynich-manuscript?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

And here is where the manuscript gets wonderfully strange.

---

# 5. What is actually pictured?

## A. Plants

There are numerous full-page botanical illustrations.

Some look vaguely like recognizable medieval plants.

Others look like:

> “Someone remembered a plant after seeing it in a dream.”

There are roots, stems, leaves, flowers and strange composite structures.

The problem is that **identifying the plants has proven extremely difficult**.

Some proposed identifications have been spectacularly specific — and subsequently challenged.

This is one of the manuscript's recurring traps:

> You can often find _something_ in a Voynich illustration that resembles something known.

But resemblance is not identification.

---

## B. Zodiac and astronomical imagery

There are:

- zodiac symbols
- stars
- circular diagrams
- suns
- moons
- constellations
- rings
- radial structures
- sequences of small human figures

Some zodiac symbols are relatively recognizable.

For example, Aries, Taurus, Gemini, Cancer, Pisces, Sagittarius, etc. can be identified with varying confidence.

But what the accompanying writing says about them remains unknown.

---

## C. The famous naked figures

This is probably the most visually unforgettable section.

Numerous **female human figures** appear:

- bathing
- standing in pools
- emerging from pools
- connected to tubes
- surrounded by green liquid
- arranged around circular structures

The drawings have often been interpreted as:

- medical
- reproductive
- anatomical
- alchemical
- astrological
- therapeutic
- symbolic

But none of those interpretations has been demonstrated conclusively.

The term **“balneological section”** comes from the apparent bathing imagery — not from a translation of the text. [The Voynich Manuscript](https://www.thevoynich.org/manuscript?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

---

# 6. The pipes are particularly intriguing

Some illustrations show what look like **tubes or channels connecting pools containing human figures**.

They look almost like plumbing.

Naturally, this generated enormous speculation.

Possible interpretations have included:

- circulation of bodily fluids
- reproductive anatomy
- medical treatment
- bathing technology
- alchemical processes
- metaphorical cosmology
- purely symbolic diagrams

But again:

**we don't know.**

This is a recurring principle with the Voynich:

> **We can describe what the illustrator drew without knowing what the illustrator meant.**

---

# 7. The giant foldout diagrams

Then we get to some of the most spectacular pages.

There are large multi-page foldouts containing:

- circles within circles
- stars
- rosettes
- strange architectural forms
- paths and connecting structures
- celestial-looking objects

The famous **Rosettes foldout** is particularly notorious.

It has been interpreted as:

- a map
- a cosmological diagram
- a city
- an astronomical system
- a metaphysical diagram
- a diagram of the universe
- an imaginary landscape

There is no consensus.

And because the pages fold out into a large connected image, the original designer clearly intended the reader to perceive relationships across multiple pages.

---

# 8. Pharmaceutical-looking pages

Another section contains drawings that resemble:

- roots
- leaves
- plant parts
- jars
- containers

Some illustrations look remarkably like a **medieval apothecary manual**.

This is one reason the medical/pharmaceutical hypothesis has remained attractive.

The final section is also particularly suggestive: dense blocks of text are preceded by little **star/flower-like markers**, giving the appearance of individual entries or recipes.

Yale describes these as possibly recipes, but carefully avoids claiming that interpretation as fact. [Yale Library](https://beinecke.library.yale.edu/beinecke/collections/beinecke-cipher-voynich-manuscript?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

---

# 9. And then there's the writing...

This is the heart of the mystery.

The manuscript contains a script that nobody has securely identified.

It has approximately a few dozen recurring glyphs, depending on how one distinguishes variants.

Today researchers often use **EVA — the European Voynich Alphabet** — to represent the characters digitally.

But EVA is **not the original alphabet**.

It's essentially a transcription convention.

That's an important distinction.

---

# 10. Why can't we decipher it?

There isn't one problem.

There are **several problems stacked on top of each other.**

### Problem 1 — No known language

We don't know what language lies underneath the writing.

Possibilities proposed over the years have included:

- Latin
- Italian
- Hebrew
- various Romance languages
- Germanic languages
- Semitic languages
- an unknown language
- a constructed language
- a ciphered language

None has achieved general acceptance.

---

### Problem 2 — No Rosetta Stone

This is enormous.

With Egyptian hieroglyphs, scholars eventually had the **Rosetta Stone**, giving the same text in multiple scripts/languages.

With Voynich:

**nothing equivalent exists.**

We have no bilingual inscription saying:

> Voynich text = Latin text

or

> Voynich symbol X = Latin A

We are trying to solve both:

**the script AND the language.**

---

### Problem 3 — We don't know whether it is encrypted

This is subtle.

There are at least several possibilities:

**A. Natural language written in an unknown script**

**B. Known language encrypted with a cipher**

**C. Natural language modified by abbreviation/shorthand**

**D. Constructed language**

**E. Some combination of these**

**F. Deliberately generated pseudo-language**

The manuscript's statistical structure has made the simplest “random gibberish” hypothesis increasingly unattractive, but that doesn't tell us what the underlying language is. Linguistic analysis has found patterns consistent with language-like structure. [Annual Reviews](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-linguistics-011619-030613?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

---

### Problem 4 — The text behaves strangely

The writing has unusual statistical properties.

For example, certain character sequences recur with remarkable regularity.

Words also tend to have constrained structures depending upon their position.

This creates an interesting paradox:

> **It looks too structured to be ordinary random nonsense, but it doesn't behave like a straightforward conventional cipher either.**

That's one of the reasons the manuscript has remained such a good cryptographic problem.

---

# 11. Who has tried to decode it?

Oh, this is where the story becomes extraordinary.

There have been **centuries of attempts**.

Let's walk through the major players.

---

# 12. Athanasius Kircher — the 17th century

The manuscript enters the historical record more clearly in the 17th century.

**Johannes Marcus Marci** sent material from the manuscript to the famous Jesuit scholar **Athanasius Kircher** in 1666.

Kircher was one of Europe's great polymaths and had already worked on Egyptian hieroglyphics and other obscure scripts.

He couldn't decipher the Voynich.

That correspondence is historically important because it establishes that the manuscript was already regarded as an incomprehensible curiosity centuries ago. [Yale Library Search](https://search.library.yale.edu/archives/11167038?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

---

# 13. Wilfrid Voynich — 1912

Voynich acquired the manuscript in 1912 from the Jesuit collection near Rome.

He immediately became fascinated by it.

He also constructed the **Roger Bacon hypothesis**.

Voynich wasn't simply trying to solve the manuscript; he was trying to establish its historical identity.

That turned out to be problematic.

Nevertheless, his discovery gave the manuscript its modern name.

---

# 14. William Romaine Newbold — the first great decoding claim

This is one of my favourite chapters of the Voynich story.

Newbold was a professor of philosophy who became convinced that the manuscript contained **microscopic shorthand hidden inside the characters**.

He claimed that when the strange characters were examined carefully, they contained tiny symbols.

From those supposed symbols he extracted elaborate messages.

His conclusion?

The manuscript contained previously unknown scientific discoveries by **Roger Bacon**.

Among other things, he claimed to find descriptions resembling:

- telescopic observations
- biological discoveries
- astronomical observations

It was an extraordinary claim.

And it was wrong.

The microscopic “shorthand” turned out to be largely an artifact of the natural irregularities of handwritten ink.

Newbold's method collapsed under scrutiny.

It became an early demonstration of a phenomenon that would repeatedly plague Voynich research:

> **If you already expect a particular answer, the manuscript contains enough ambiguity for you to find it.**

---

# 15. William and Elizebeth Friedman

Now we get serious cryptography.

**William Friedman** is one of the giants of modern cryptanalysis.

He and his wife **Elizebeth Friedman** were among the most important American cryptologists of the 20th century.

William Friedman became deeply interested in the Voynich.

A group of experienced cryptanalysts worked on it.

Their conclusion was fascinating:

> The manuscript probably wasn't simply a straightforward medieval substitution cipher.

Friedman's famous inclination was that the manuscript represented an **artificial or constructed language**, rather than simply encrypted Latin.

No reproducible decipherment emerged.

The NSA later produced Mary D'Imperio's important study:

**The Voynich Manuscript — An Elegant Enigma**

which became one of the most influential modern surveys of the subject. [Yale News](https://news.yale.edu/2016/10/31/mysterious-voynich-manuscript-reborn-facsimile-edition?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

---

# 16. John Tiltman

British cryptanalyst **John Tiltman**, another exceptionally accomplished codebreaker, also examined the manuscript.

Again:

No solution.

And this matters.

Because when highly experienced cryptanalysts with enormous practical experience in real-world codebreaking can't establish a key, it tells us that the Voynich isn't simply a badly presented Caesar cipher waiting for someone clever enough to notice.

---

# 17. Prescott Currier — an enormously important breakthrough

Currier's contribution wasn't a decoding.

It was something more valuable:

**description.**

He discovered that the manuscript contains two statistically distinct varieties of text.

They became known as:

### Currier A

and

### Currier B

He also found differences in handwriting and vocabulary distribution.

This helped establish that the manuscript wasn't necessarily one homogeneous stream of writing.

That was a major advance because it shifted the question from:

> “What does this alphabet mean?”

toward:

> “How was this manuscript actually constructed?” [Voynich Manuscript](https://www.voynich.nu/extra/curr_main.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

---

# 18. Mary D'Imperio

D'Imperio's 1978 NSA study deserves special mention.

She brought together:

- cryptography
- linguistics
- statistics
- manuscript studies
- historical analysis

and critically reviewed the major competing theories.

Her work became a foundation for later researchers.

The NSA eventually declassified it, and Yale notes it as one of the important works in the manuscript's modern research history. [Yale News](https://news.yale.edu/2016/10/31/mysterious-voynich-manuscript-reborn-facsimile-edition?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

---

# 19. Gordon Rugg — perhaps the most provocative alternative

In the early 2000s, computer scientist **Gordon Rugg** proposed something radically different.

What if the manuscript wasn't encrypted text at all?

What if it was **manufactured pseudo-text**?

Rugg investigated whether a simple mechanism such as a **Cardan grille** could generate Voynich-like word patterns.

His experiments showed that text with many of the manuscript's statistical characteristics could indeed be generated artificially.

This didn't prove that the Voynich was fake.

But it produced a very important methodological warning:

> **Statistical “language-like” behaviour does not automatically prove that a text encodes meaningful language.**

That distinction remains relevant today.

---

# 20. Stephen Bax — 2014

Linguist **Stephen Bax** took a different approach.

Instead of trying to crack the entire manuscript, he attempted to identify individual words using the illustrations.

For example:

> If this picture is a particular plant, perhaps the adjacent word represents that plant.

He proposed several tentative readings.

It was an interesting **partial-decoding strategy**.

But it never developed into a reproducible full decipherment.

Bax himself was considerably more cautious than many later media headlines about “decoding the Voynich.” [Annual Reviews](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-linguistics-011619-030613?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

---

# 21. Greg Kondrak and Bradley Hauer — computational language identification

In 2018, computer scientists **Greg Kondrak and Bradley Hauer** used computational linguistic techniques.

They proposed that the manuscript might be related to **Hebrew**, after algorithmically comparing its text with language corpora.

This attracted considerable attention.

But an important distinction emerged:

> Language identification ≠ decipherment.

Even Kondrak did not claim that their statistical result constituted a complete translation.

It did not produce a reproducible reading of the manuscript.

---

# 22. Nicholas Gibbs — 2017

Gibbs proposed that the manuscript was essentially a **medical/health text for women**, written in abbreviated Latin.

This was attractive because it matched some of the illustrations.

But specialists pointed out serious problems with the proposed readings and methodology.

Again:

**interesting hypothesis — not a demonstrated decipherment.**

---

# 23. Gerard Cheshire — 2019

This one generated a particularly dramatic media story.

Cheshire claimed that the manuscript represented a previously unidentified **“proto-Romance” language** and that he had deciphered portions of it.

The claim attracted substantial press attention.

But the academic response was highly critical, and the University of Bristol subsequently removed/withdrew the associated university press material.

It did not produce an accepted decipherment. [El Enigma Voynich](https://elenigmavoynich.com/en/voynich-questions/has-the-voynich-manuscript-been-deciphered-in-2026?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

---

# 24. The 2025 Caspari–Faccini claim

And this brings us very close to the present.

In 2025, **Gino Caspari and Agnese Faccini** published a preprint titled:

**“A Key to the Voynich Manuscript.”**

They proposed:

- **vernacular Italian**
- written in **shorthand**
- with a partially decoded alphabet
- and claimed a substantial vocabulary could be read consistently.

Their abstract explicitly says they identified the language as vernacular Italian and provide a lexical list of more than 400 words. [Max Planck Institute](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_3644902/component/file_3644903/content?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This is an important claim to know about because it is not merely an internet forum theory.

However:

### It has not been accepted as a decipherment.

A 2026 quantitative test reconstructed their proposed key and reported that it **did not confirm** the proposed solution. [Voynich Studies](https://voynich.site/en/hypotheses/caspari-faccini-key/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

So at present I would classify it as:

**serious research claim → interesting → independently testable → not validated.**

---

# 25. There are even 2026 “decipherments”

And this is where we have to be particularly careful.

There are currently projects claiming:

- complete or near-complete decoding
- Latin/Occitan pharmaceutical text
- positional substitution
- specific grammar
- extremely high percentages of decoded tokens
- reproducible computational decipherment

For example, one current project claims **88.5% token coverage** and a positional homophonic cipher. [Voynich Decoded](https://www.voynich-decoded.com/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Another 2026 project presents a claimed pre-registered candidate decipherment with statistical probabilities supporting its proposed grammar. [The Voynich Project](https://thevoynichproject.com/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

And another current research project reports a proposed **Naibbe-class homophonic cipher** as a model capable of reproducing multiple statistical properties of Voynichese. [Voynich Studies](https://voynich.site/?lang=en&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

These are interesting developments.

But we should distinguish:

> **“A model can reproduce the statistical characteristics of Voynichese.”**

from:

> **“We know what Voynichese means.”**

Those are very different achievements.

---

# 26. So has anyone actually decoded it?

### No — not in the scholarly sense.

This is the single most important answer.

As of September 2026, there is **no generally accepted translation of the Voynich Manuscript**. [El Enigma Voynich](https://elenigmavoynich.com/en/voynich-questions/has-the-voynich-manuscript-been-deciphered-in-2026?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

A genuine decipherment would need to demonstrate something like:

1. a consistent mapping/rule
2. coherent language
3. meaningful grammar
4. reproducible readings
5. applicability to previously unseen portions
6. correspondence with the illustrations where appropriate
7. independent researchers able to reproduce the result

And preferably:

8. publication and serious peer review.

No proposed solution has cleared that entire hurdle.

---

# 27. Why do the “decoded” claims keep appearing?

This is perhaps the most interesting meta-question.

The Voynich is almost a perfect machine for generating false positives.

Imagine you have:

**~37,000 tokens**

written in an unknown script,

with unknown language,

unknown direction of encoding,

unknown grammar,

unknown cultural context,

and pictures that are themselves ambiguous.

You can construct thousands of hypotheses.

Eventually one will produce:

> “This word resembles Latin for plant.”

or:

> “This looks like Hebrew.”

or:

> “This resembles Italian.”

or:

> “This symbol corresponds to the Sun.”

The danger is **confirmation bias**.

A proposed solution must not merely explain the examples the researcher selected.

It must predict things the researcher **didn't use to construct the theory.**

That's the gold standard.

# 28. What _has_ actually been learned?

Quite a lot.

And this is where I think the Voynich becomes more fascinating than the popular “unsolved mystery” framing suggests.

### Physical facts

We know:

- it is genuinely old vellum
- the parchment dates to the early 15th century
- the material isn't some modern fabrication
- the pigments and inks are consistent with historical manuscript production
- it was constructed as a real codex
- it has missing leaves
- it contains elaborate foldouts
- it was produced by skilled scribal/bookmaking processes

The McCrone materials analysis found ordinary historical manuscript materials rather than some exotic modern substance. [Yale Library](https://beinecke.library.yale.edu/sites/default/files/files/voynich_analysis.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

---

### Textual facts

Researchers have established:

- recurring glyphs
- recurring word forms
- positional structure
- unusual statistical regularities
- section-specific vocabulary
- differences between Currier A and B
- relationships between illustrations and textual regions
- repeated structures across the manuscript

So we aren't staring at complete chaos.

---

### Linguistic facts

Statistical linguistics has found that the text displays several properties compatible with **structured language**.

The important scholarly review by Bowern and Lindemann argues that the manuscript should be treated seriously as a linguistic object rather than simply dismissed as medieval gibberish. [Annual Reviews](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-linguistics-011619-030613?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

But:

> **language-like structure does not prove a particular language.**

I'd divide what we know into four layers:

### 🟢 Very strong evidence

- medieval/early Renaissance physical object
- vellum
- early-15th-century parchment
- genuine historical pigments/ink
- real codex construction
- real unknown script
- real illustrations
- real missing leaves
- real historical ownership trail from the 17th century onward

### 🟡 Strong scholarly observations

- multiple textual varieties

- repeated word patterns
- meaningful-looking section differences

### 🟠 Plausible but unresolved

- medical/pharmaceutical purpose
- astronomical/astrological purpose
- herbal knowledge
- multiple scribes

- complete pharmaceutical translation

- any specific “I have completely decoded it” announcement

# 31. And there is one especially beautiful paradox

The Voynich Manuscript may actually be **more valuable because we cannot read it**.

If tomorrow someone produced a genuinely validated translation, it would become a fascinating historical document.

But right now it sits at a rare intersection of:

**codicology + palaeography + cryptography + linguistics + statistics + medieval medicine + botany + astronomy + information theory + computer science + psychology.**

Every generation brings a new tool.

The early scholars brought **philology**.

Newbold brought **microscopic analysis**.

Friedman brought **modern cryptanalysis**.

Currier brought **statistical textual analysis**.

Rugg brought **computational generation**.

Modern researchers bring **machine learning, corpus linguistics and computational hypothesis testing**.

And yet the central question remains:

> **What does this book actually say?**

---

## One final distinction I think is important

There are actually **three different Voynich problems**:

**Problem A — What is the object?**

This is largely solved.

**Problem B — How was the text generated?**

We are making real progress here.

**Problem C — What does the text mean?**

That remains unsolved.

And I think **B is currently the most promising route toward C**.

Instead of asking an AI:

> _“Translate this mysterious language.”_

the more rigorous question is:

> **“What process could have generated these exact statistical, scribal and positional characteristics?”**

Once we understand the _mechanism that generated the text_, we may finally have a foothold on the language underneath it.

That is a much more interesting problem than simply trying to guess what a strange-looking word means.
